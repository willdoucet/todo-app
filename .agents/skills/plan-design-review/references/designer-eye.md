# The designer's eye

Loaded by `plan-design-review` at step 5. These are not a checklist; they are how you see. The
perceptual instincts that separate "looked at the design" from "understood why it feels wrong".
Let them run automatically while you review, rate, and fix.

## Cognitive patterns

1. **Seeing the system, not the screen.** Never evaluate a screen in isolation: what comes
   before it, what comes after, what happens when things break.
2. **Empathy as simulation.** Not "I feel for the user" but running the simulations: bad
   signal, one hand free, the boss watching, the first time versus the thousandth time.
3. **Hierarchy as service.** Every decision answers "what should the user see first, second,
   third?" Respecting their time, not prettifying pixels.
4. **Constraint worship.** Limitations force clarity. "If I can only show three things, which
   three matter most?"
5. **The question reflex.** The first instinct is questions, not opinions. "Who is this for?
   What did they try before this?"
6. **Edge-case paranoia.** What if the name is 47 characters? Zero results? The network fails?
   The user is colorblind? The language runs right to left?
7. **The "would I notice?" test.** Invisible is perfect. The highest compliment is not noticing
   the design.
8. **Principled taste.** "This feels wrong" is traceable to a broken principle. Taste is
   debuggable, not subjective. Julie Zhuo: a great designer defends her work based on
   principles that last.
9. **Subtraction by default.** "As little design as possible" (Dieter Rams). "Subtract the
   obvious, add the meaningful" (John Maeda).
10. **Time-horizon design.** The first five seconds (visceral), the first five minutes
    (behavioral), the five-year relationship (reflective). Design for all three at once
    (Don Norman, Emotional Design).
11. **Design for trust.** Every decision builds or erodes trust. Strangers sharing a home
    requires pixel-level intentionality about safety, identity, and belonging (Joe Gebbia,
    Airbnb).
12. **Storyboard the journey.** Before touching pixels, storyboard the full emotional arc.
    Gebbia's "Snow White" method: every moment is a scene with a mood, not a screen with a
    layout.

## Key references

- Dieter Rams, ten principles of good design.
- Don Norman, three levels of design: visceral, behavioral, reflective.
- Jakob Nielsen, ten usability heuristics.
- Gestalt principles: proximity, similarity, closure, continuity.
- Ira Glass on the gap: your taste is why your work disappoints you.
- Jony Ive: people can sense care and can sense carelessness; different and new is relatively
  easy, genuinely better is very hard.
- Joe Gebbia: designing for trust between strangers; storyboarding emotional journeys.

## How they apply during the review

- While reading the plan, empathy as simulation runs automatically: for every screen, run the
  bad-signal, one-hand, first-time, and thousandth-time cases and note what the plan says
  about each. Silence is a finding.
- While rating, principled taste makes the score debuggable. Every point below 10 names the
  principle that costs it.
- When something seems cluttered, apply subtraction before proposing additions. Ask what can
  be removed before asking what is missing.
- When the plan describes a layout without a story, storyboard first (pass 3), then judge the
  layout against the story.
- When a decision is left to the implementer, the question reflex turns it into a question for
  the user now (pass 7), not a guess later.
